Remote Access

At home, Melon just works: anything on the same network can find it. Remote Access is for listening when you're out, on mobile data or at a friend's place. Melon can set it up for you, no domain name or port forwarding guide needed, and nothing runs on anyone else's server.

Before you turn it on

Remote Access means Melon can be reached from the internet, not just your house. Melon is built to be secure, but nothing is perfect, and a bug nobody knows about yet could let someone use Melon to get into your computer. While it's on, Melon keeps Security Logs of who's connecting and what they're sending, and suggests blocking addresses that look sketchy. More on that below.

Setting it up

Open Networking in MelonGUI or MelonCLI and pick Set up Remote Access. You'll get the warning above, then Melon checks your network for a few seconds. It looks at:

  • Your public address, the one the internet sees.
  • CGNAT: some internet providers share one public address between lots of customers. If yours does, a router port can't help, since the port you'd need isn't yours.
  • Double NAT: two routers in a row, like your provider's modem-router plus your own.
  • Automatic port opening: whether your router lets apps open a port by themselves (UPnP or NAT-PMP).
  • IPv6: whether it works on your connection.
  • Dynamic or static address: whether your public address changes now and then. Most home connections do.

Then you get three switches. Each one is marked Recommended, Required, Optional or Not available based on what Melon found, with a line saying why. If you're not sure, go with what it recommends.

Open your router port

Melon asks your router to open a port to it, so apps can reach it from outside. This is the main way in for most people. If your router won't do it on its own (lots don't, or have it turned off), Melon tells you on the Networking page, and you can forward the port by hand in your router's settings: TCP port 14525 to this computer's home address, unless you changed the port.

IPv6

Only shows up if IPv6 works on your network. IPv6 gives every device its own public address, which gets around CGNAT and double NAT. If you're stuck behind CGNAT, this is usually the one that saves you. The catch: the phone has to have IPv6 too, which most mobile networks do, but not every Wi-Fi network does.

One more catch: most routers block incoming IPv6 connections until you allow them. If phones can't connect over IPv6, look for an IPv6 firewall (or "pinhole") setting in your router and allow TCP port 14525 to this computer. Testing from the same computer doesn't count, it never leaves your house. Try a phone on mobile data with Wi-Fi off.

Pkarr

If your public address changes, apps lose track of Melon until they're back on your home network. With Pkarr on, Melon publishes its current address, signed with its own key, to a big shared network (the same one BitTorrent uses for finding peers). Apps look it up there when Melon's address changes. Nobody can fake the record, since apps check the signature against the key from the QR code.

Leave it off and things still work, you'll just sometimes have to bring a phone home to resync it.

Pick Turn on and you're done. The Networking page shows whether it's working, and it says so once the first app connects from outside.

Advanced setup

The same screen has Advanced setup, for other ways in:

  • Reverse proxy: you run something like Caddy in front of Melon with your own domain. Enter the address apps should use, like https://music.example.com. The Caddy guide walks through it.
  • Tailscale: if Tailscale is installed and connected, Melon can use it. Nothing is open to the internet at all, but every phone needs Tailscale too. Good if you're behind CGNAT with no IPv6.
  • Custom listening URL and certificate: the old manual setup. See Installation.

Security logs and blocking

While Remote Access is on, the Networking page has a Security section. It shows who's connected from outside, what's been happening, and suggestions when an address looks off (lots of failed sign-ins, poking at random paths, that kind of thing). Each suggestion asks if you want to block it, yes or no. You can also block an address by hand, and unblock it again from the blocked list.

In the apps

Galia and Watermelon show whether Remote Access is on and how it's set up, but they can't change it. That only happens on the computer running Melon, so a stolen phone or sign-in can't open your server up.

Apps save every address Melon gives them and try them all at once, so they switch between home and away by themselves. When your phone moves between Wi-Fi and mobile data, they check again within a few seconds. If none of the addresses work, they look on the local network, then ask Pkarr.

Inviting people

Invite QR codes and links carry Melon's addresses too, so a friend can sign up from their own house. That only works with Remote Access on. With it off, the invite only works on your home network, and MelonGUI and MelonCLI tell you so when you make one.

Turning it off

Turn off Remote Access on the Networking page closes the router port, stops publishing to Pkarr, and goes back to home-only. Apps keep working at home.
